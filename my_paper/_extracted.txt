Abstract. In-context learning (ICL) for relation extraction is often un-
dermined by its reliance on implicit heuristics. This weakness is critical
in two stages: (1) example selection, where semantic similarity serves
as a poor proxy for utility; (2) model reasoning, which depends on un-
guided attention mechanisms. This reliance on non-optimizable strate-
gies leads to unreliable performance and hinders interpretability. This
paper advocates for a fundamental shift from implicit heuristics to ex-
plicit optimization and guidance. We introduce CLARE (Contribution-
driven Learning and Adaptive REasoning), a unified framework that
introduces core innovations to both the example selection and model
reasoning stages. For example selection, we propose Predictive Con-
tribution Estimation (PCE), a novel method that trains a retriever
to directly optimize for an example’s utility by quantifying its impact
on the model’s prediction confidence. For model reasoning, we introduce
Adaptive Inference Modulation (AIM), which transforms the un-
guided inference process into a steerable one by dynamically modulating
the model’s internal attention scores. This ensures that the most valu-
able demonstration information is precisely leveraged. Experiments on
four challenging datasets validate that CLARE significantly outperforms
mainstream ICL baselines by leveraging explicit contribution estimation
and guided inference.
Keywords: Relation Extraction · In-context Learning · Contrastive Learn-
ing · Attention.

Related Work
In-Context Learning (ICL) for relation extraction is powerful yet fragile, with
its performance critically dependent on two stages still governed by implicit
heuristics: example selection and model inference [8]. Our work challenges this
paradigm by introducing explicit, optimization-driven mechanisms for both.
2.1
From Heuristic to Optimized Example Selection.
The dominant strategy for example selection relies on semantic similarity, ex-
emplified by SimCSE [5], an unreliable proxy for an example’s true utility. Rec-
ognizing this limitation, more sophisticated heuristics have emerged. These in-
clude designing task-aware retrievers (GPT-RE [18]), employing dual-systems
(DSARE [9]), or using complex feedback loops to verify reasoning and re-select
examples (SRVF [7]). Other methods, like GL [14], augment the prompt with
learned guidelines. Despite their ingenuity, these approaches remain fundamen-
tally heuristic. Even more advanced paradigms that train a dedicated retriever
[16,21] or leverage direct LLM feedback for preference learning (GenICL [23])
still rely on indirect, coarse-grained, or relative optimization signals (e.g., ’A is
better than B’). These approaches fail to directly quantify an individual exam-
ple’s intrinsic, absolute utility for a given query.
Our work proposes a different, non-preference-based approach. Our Predic-
tive Contribution Estimation (PCE) module is designed to quantify an example’s

absolute, quantifiable utility (its predictive gain). This allows us to train a re-
triever using a supervised, contrastive framework that explicitly distinguishes
between demonstrably helpful and harmful examples, rather than just learning
their relative order.
2.2
From Black-Box to Steerable Inference.
The second critical weakness of ICL is treating the LLM’s inference process as
a black box. The standard approach of simply concatenating examples relies
on the model’s unguided attention mechanisms to distill relevant information
[18,13]. This is often suboptimal, as noisy demonstrations can mislead the model,
sometimes resulting in performance worse than a zero-shot baseline [15]. While
frameworks like SRVF [7] attempt to verify the model’s reasoning post-hoc, they
do not make the internal inference process itself transparent or steerable. A key
research gap remains in developing methods that can actively guide the model’s
focus on salient information within examples [19].
Our Adaptive Inference Modulation (AIM) module directly addresses this
gap. It transforms the unguided inference process into a transparent and control-
lable one by explicitly modulating the model’s internal attention scores, ensuring
that the most valuable demonstration information is precisely leveraged.
3
Methodology
In-context learning (ICL) for relation extraction is fundamentally constrained
by its reliance on two implicit and unguided processes: example selection and
inference. To address these limitations, we propose CLARE, a unified frame-
work designed to systematically replace these implicit heuristics with explicit,
optimizable modules. As illustrated in Figure 2, our framework consists of two
key stages: (1) Explicit Modeling of Example Contribution for retrieval, and (2)
Explicit Guidance of the Inference Process.
3.1
Task Formulation
We first formulate the standard ICL-based RE task. Given a query xq and a large
candidate pool of available examples C, the initial goal is to select an ordered set
of N demonstrations E = {e1, . . . , eN} where ei ∈C. These demonstrations are
then formatted and concatenated with the query to form a single prompt, P =
(e1, . . . , eN, xq). This prompt is fed to a pre-trained LLM, which autoregressively
generates the relation y. The objective is to maximize the conditional probability
of the true relation ytrue:
argmax P(ytrue|e1, . . . , eN, xq)
(1)
The overall performance critically depends on two independent sub-problems: (1)
the selection mechanism for choosing the optimal set E, and (2) the inference
process by which the model utilizes E to compute the probability. Our work
introduces explicit optimization for both.

3.2
Predictive Contribution Estimation (PCE)
The effectiveness of ICL hinges on example quality, but this process is typically
governed by the implicit heuristic of semantic similarity—an unreliable proxy
for an example’s true utility. To replace this heuristic with a principled opti-
mization target, we introduce Predictive Contribution Estimation (PCE),
a framework for training a retriever on an explicit objective. The core idea is to
directly measure an example’s value by its quantifiable impact on the model’s
predictive performance, as detailed in Figure 2 (left).
Quantifying Example Contribution. Conventional heuristics like relation
label consistency are flawed, as they fail to capture an example’s true utility.
To overcome this, we propose quantifying contribution based on its direct impact
on the LLM’s predictive behavior. We introduce the Predictive Contribution
Score (PCS), an explicit metric measuring the change in the model’s confidence
in the correct label ytrue when presented with a candidate example ec.
Formally, for a given query xq, its PCS is defined as:
PCS(ec, xq) = P(ytrue|ec, xq) −P(ytrue|xq)
(2)
where:
– P(ytrue|xq) is the baseline probability (zero-shot).
– P(ytrue|ec, xq) is the probability (one-shot) with ec as context.

Experiment
4.1
Experimental Setup
Datasets. We conduct experiments on four widely-recognized relation extrac-
tion benchmarks: SemEval-2010 Task 8 [6], SciERC [12], ACE05 [17], and TA-
CRED [22]. We use Micro-F1 as the primary evaluation metric across all datasets.
– SemEval-2010 Task 8 is a seminal benchmark for sentence-level relation
extraction, containing 9 directed semantic relations and an "Other" category.
– SciERC is sourced from scientific abstracts, featuring 7 relation types and
presenting the challenge of cross-sentence relations.
– ACE05 is a complex dataset derived from diverse sources, defining 7 coarse-
grained entity relation types.
– TACRED is a large-scale dataset with 41 fine-grained relation types from
the political domain. Since over 80% of instances are negative ("no_relation"),
we follow standard protocols and report Micro-F1 scores exclusively on pos-
itive relation types.

– Random selects in-context demonstrations from the labeled samples, serv-

– GL [14] proposes Guideline Learning (GL), which learns from errors to create

– GPT-RE [18] enhances in-context learning through task-aware retrieval and

Implementation Details. We employ Meta-Llama-3-8B-Instruct [3] as our
backbone model for the final relation prediction. For the retriever, we initialize
its encoder with RoBERTa-large [10]. All experiments are conducted on a server
equipped with eight NVIDIA RTX 3090 Ti GPUs. The retriever is trained for
400 epochs using the AdamW [11] optimizer with a learning rate of 1e-6. We use
a batch size of 1 and 4 gradient accumulation steps.
To build the PCE training set, we identified low-confidence queries and com-
puted their PCS scores against a candidate pool. To address the computational
cost of this initial phase, we applied a downsampling strategy: for each query, we
computed scores against 2,000 randomly sampled candidates rather than the full
dataset. We opted for random sampling over coarse semantic filtering to ensure
the retriever learns from a diverse range of utility distributions, preventing bias
towards semantic similarity. Although building this set requires LLM forward
passes, it is a one-time offline cost that enables a lightweight, highly efficient re-
triever for all future inference, ensuring practical scalability. The threshold δ for
constructing positive and negative samples is set to 0.01, and the temperature
parameter τ for contrastive learning is 0.05.
To constrain the output format and improve prediction reliability, we append
the template The correct answer is: ** to the model’s input. The model’s pre-
diction is then determined by the logits of the token immediately following this
template. To compute label probabilities for PCS, we apply a softmax function
over the logits of only the valid relation label tokens for that specific task.
During inference, we generate 3 randomly shuffled candidate example orders
for each sample, and the final prediction is derived from the mean of these logits
across the 3 orders. For a fair comparison, this 3-shuffle ensembling strategy was
applied to all methods. For CLARE, the AIM module, including its positional
decay, was re-computed independently for each of the 3 permutations.

4.2
Main Results
Table 2 presents the main experimental results on four benchmark datasets. Our
proposed framework, CLARE, consistently demonstrates superior performance,
achieving the highest average Micro-F1 score of 61.91 across all benchmarks. This
result validates the effectiveness of our explicit, optimization-driven approach for
in-context relation extraction.
Compared to the baselines, CLARE shows significant improvements. It sur-
passes the Random selection strategy by a large margin (an average of +9.85
F1 points), underscoring the necessity of a principled example selection method.
More importantly, CLARE outperforms SimCSE, a strong baseline relying on
semantic similarity, on three out of four datasets. This highlights the core limi-
tation of using similarity as a proxy for utility and validates our hypothesis that
directly optimizing for predictive contribution yields more effective demonstra-
tions. Notably, SimCSE is the top performer on ACE05, outperforming all other
methods including ours. We hypothesize this is due to the unique characteristics
of the dataset itself. ACE05 defines only 7 coarse-grained relation types (e.g.,
PHYS for physical, PER-SOC for personal-social), where high semantic or top-
ical similarity—which SimCSE excels at capturing—is often a reliable proxy for
an example’s true utility.
This scenario, where the proxy heuristic (similarity) aligns well with the
true objective (utility), may explain why SimCSE excels. However, CLARE’s
superior and consistent performance on the other three datasets, especially its
state-of-the-art results on the fine-grained TACRED (64.02) and SciERC (65.71),
validates its robustness. It demonstrates that CLARE excels precisely on tasks
where the semantic heuristic fails and a more explicit, contribution-driven model
is necessary.

summary, all three components of AIM are integral to its success.
4.5
Analysis of Positive Example Composition
To validate our 1-shot-based sample construction, we evaluated whether the pos-
itive effect of examples generalizes when they are composed in n-shot contexts.
We conducted an experiment on the SemEval dataset, measuring the consistency
of prompts composed of 2, 5, 10, and 20 examples previously identified as posi-
tive. Consistency is defined as the combined prompt still improving the correct
label’s logit score.
The results demonstrate remarkable robustness. The consistency rate de-
clines gracefully from 94.7% (2-shot) to a strong 80.9% even in a dense 20-shot
scenario. This gradual and expected decrease, caused by rising inter-example
noise in larger contexts, confirms that our 1-shot identification strategy is fun-
damentally sound. It successfully provides a reliable data foundation for training
a retriever that identifies examples robustly beneficial in realistic, multi-example
settings.

4.6
Empirical Validation of Targeted Head Identification
To validate our data-driven approach for identifying critical attention heads, we
analyzed their selection frequency on the SemEval test set using Llama3-8B-Instruct.
We designated the top 10% of heads in each layer as critical based on their Query-
Context Interaction Scores and aggregated the counts across all test cases.
The resulting heatmap (Figure 3a) reveals two crucial patterns. First, within
any given layer, selection is highly concentrated on a small, consistent subset of
heads, confirming functional specialization. Second, and more critically, this set
of important heads is dynamic and changes across different layers. This strongly
indicates a hierarchical specialization, where the model leverages different heads
for reasoning at different processing depths.
This finding powerfully justifies AIM’s core design: a targeted, layer-specific
intervention is not merely an optimization but a necessity to adapt to the evolv-
ing functional roles of attention heads throughout the network.
5
Conclusion
In-context learning (ICL) for relation extraction is often undermined by its re-
liance on implicit heuristics. This paper identified two critical bottlenecks—heuristic
example selection based on flawed proxies like semantic similarity, and unguided
model reasoning. We argued for a paradigm shift from these implicit heuristics
to explicit modeling and guidance.
To this end, we introduced CLARE, a unified framework with two syn-
ergistic components. First, Predictive Contribution Estimation (PCE) trains a
retriever to directly optimize for an example’s true utility by quantifying its
predictive gain. Second, Adaptive Inference Modulation (AIM) transforms the
black-box inference process into a steerable one by dynamically modulating the
model’s internal attention, ensuring that the high-quality examples selected by
PCE are precisely leveraged.
Extensive experiments on four datasets demonstrated that our explicit, op-
timization and guidance approach allows CLARE to consistently outperform
strong ICL baselines. This work validates the effectiveness of replacing fragile
heuristics with principled, optimizable mechanisms and opens new avenues for
building more robust, controllable, and interpretable in-context learning systems.
